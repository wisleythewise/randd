# Container Unloading: A Reading List That Actually Survives Contact with a Warehouse

## Why I'm Writing This

Picking a research direction for a real-world container-unloading deployment
is mostly an exercise in *not* being seduced by the most papers, the most
citations, or the most recent benchmark numbers. The literature on this
problem is bimodal: there's a large pile of deep-learning grasp-prediction
work that runs beautifully on the Cornell or YCB datasets and disappoints
when you bolt it onto a UR20 in a 40-foot reefer, and a smaller pile of
mechanical engineering papers that look unfashionable, get fewer citations,
and quietly underpin every production system that actually ships. This
braindump is about finding the second pile.

The constraint I'm imposing here is twofold. First, every paper has to be
freely downloadable as a PDF — no Springer-paywalled chapters that promise
to be perfect and turn out to be a four-page summary, no ResearchGate
"request full text" stubs. Second, I'm preferring papers that lean
analytical, model-based, or classical-CV first, with deep learning treated
as an optional layer rather than the substrate. This isn't a religious
position; it's because for a real-world deployment we want the failure modes
to be debuggable on a warehouse floor at 2 AM by an automation engineer who
needs to know *why* a pick failed, not just retrain a network with more data.

## The Single Most Realistic Paper

If I could only read one paper before kicking off the project, it would be
**[Grasp Failure Constraints for Fast and Reliable Pick-and-Place Using
Multi-Suction-Cup Grippers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.03498)** (2024).

The reason is almost embarrassingly practical. Production container-unload
end-effectors are not single suction cups; they're arrays of four to twelve
cups arranged on a manifold, often with independent valves so you can
selectively activate the cups that have a seal. The interesting engineering
question for that geometry is not "where do I grasp" — usually you slap the
gripper flush against a box face — but "given the box is on a vertical wall,
sometimes leaning, sometimes wet from condensation, with this load
distribution across cups, will the grasp survive the acceleration profile of
my retraction trajectory?" Get that wrong and you drop a 25 kg parcel on a
conveyor belt at speed, which is the failure mode that gets you fired.

This paper formulates that question as a quadratic program based on minimum
spring potential energy. Each suction cup is modeled as a Voigt-style
spring, the box as a rigid body, and the load distribution at any pose and
any acceleration vector falls out of a closed-form QP that runs in
milliseconds. That QP becomes a *constraint* in the robot's time-optimal
trajectory planner. The output is a trajectory that's as fast as physically
possible without any cup exceeding its allowable normal/shear force.

There are several things I love about this. There's no training data — none.
You measure each cup's stiffness and max force on a bench once, plug in the
box mass, and the model does the rest. The compute fits comfortably inside
a control loop running at hundreds of Hertz. When something fails on the
warehouse floor, you can step into the math and figure out exactly which
cup overloaded, which is the kind of debuggability that an "our model
predicted 0.42 grasp confidence" CNN cannot give you. And it composes
cleanly with whatever pose-estimation pipeline you choose — the QP only
needs the box geometry and the gripper-relative grasp pose.

The thing I don't love is that the experimental section is shorter than I'd
like and it doesn't directly tackle container-unloading scenes. But the
math is right, the assumptions are conservative, and you can validate it on
your own bench in a week. It is the most "deployable as written" paper in
this whole pile.

## The Foundational Paper That Predates It

The lineage of analytical suction modeling traces straight back to **[Dex-Net
3.0: Computing Robust Vacuum Suction Grasp Targets in Point Clouds](https://goldberg.berkeley.edu/pubs/ICRA2018-jeff-suction-grasping-final.pdf)**
(Mahler, Goldberg et al., ICRA 2018). The CNN side of Dex-Net got most of
the press, but the actually-load-bearing contribution is the *robust suction
contact model*: a quasi-static spring model where the cup's circular lip is
discretized into a ring of springs, the seal forms only if every spring is
in contact and within its compliance budget, and the wrench resistance set
falls out analytically. Once you have that, the CNN is just a fast
approximator that takes a depth image and outputs which pixels are likely
to satisfy the analytical model.

For deployment purposes the analytical scoring alone is often enough. I
know of at least one production company that runs the Dex-Net 3.0 contact
model directly on segmented box faces, no CNN, with very acceptable
performance. The 2024 multi-cup paper above is essentially a generalization
of this work to gripper arrays.

A useful companion document is the **[Berkeley Technical Report
EECS-2019-119](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2019/EECS-2019-119.pdf)**
(Mahler, 2019), which is the long-form treatment of suction grasp learning
as it evolved from Dex-Net. It's freely available, walks through the
analytical model in more pedagogical detail than the conference paper, and
includes failure-mode analysis from real bin-picking experiments.

## The Vision Side, With a Bias Toward "Implementable"

For container unloading you absolutely need to know where the boxes are.
The reasonable methods sit on a spectrum from "RANSAC plane fitting on
point clouds" to "instance-segmenting transformer." For a deployment
project I'd go classical first.

The **[Stacked Carton Dataset (SCD)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.12808.pdf)**
paper (2021) is unavoidable. It's a deep-learning paper — they train a
RetinaNet variant — but the dataset itself is the contribution. 250,000
instance masks across 16,000 real warehouse images of stacked cartons. If
you want to ground-truth a classical CV pipeline, validate a depth-based
segmentation, or even just calibrate your expectations about the visual
diversity of the problem, this dataset is the reference. Use the dataset
even if you don't use their model.

For a more directly traditional take, **[Machine Vision-Assisted Design of
End Effector Pose in Robotic Mixed Depalletizing of Heterogeneous
Cargo](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11858875/)** (Sensors,
2025) is more in the classical-CV-plus-geometry camp and is open access via
PubMed Central. The title is a mouthful but the content is exactly what it
says on the tin: how do you choose the gripper pose given depth-camera data
and a heterogeneous stack of boxes. Read it as a how-to.

## Where Deep Learning Earns Its Keep — But Sparingly

I'd be lying if I said deep learning has nothing to contribute here. Two
papers are worth keeping on the radar even with a traditional bias.

**[Learning Suction Graspability Considering Grasp Quality and Robot
Reachability for Bin-Picking](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8987443/)**
(Sensors, 2022) is the paper to cite when someone in a meeting says
"reachability is just an offline lookup, why do we need a network?" The
contribution is that they jointly score grasp quality *and* whether the arm
can actually reach that pose given the current scene clutter. Inside a
20-foot container with the arm 1500 mm from the back wall, an enormous
fraction of analytically-scored "good" grasps are kinematically infeasible.
This paper acknowledges the constraint and bakes it into the scoring
function. Even if you don't use their network, the formulation is
instructive.

**[FoundationPose](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.08344)** (NVIDIA, 2023) is
the current SOTA for 6D pose estimation of novel objects. If your boxes
have machine-readable labels or known SKUs with CAD models, FoundationPose
is the strongest available perception layer in May 2026. It's
deep-learning-heavy and needs a GPU to run online, so it's the optional
high-end path, not the default.

The other deep-learning paper from my search list, **[AI-Enhanced Parcel
Detecting with Adaptive 3D Machine Vision and RGB-D Imaging for Automated
Unloading](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10935264/)** (Sensors,
2024), is squarely on-topic for our problem. It's open-access, recent, and
explicitly about the unloading scenario. The "AI-enhanced" framing is real
— there's a CNN — but the pipeline is hybridized with classical edge
detection and depth processing in a way that makes the failure modes
inspectable.

## What I'm Deliberately Not Including

There's a Springer chapter from 2022 called "Robots Working in the
Backroom: Depalletization of Mixed-Case Pallets" that's the closest thing
to a production retrospective from Amazon Robotics, but it's paywalled and
the user-side rule is "PDF or it doesn't count." The ScienceDirect paper
on A* sequence planning for container unload is similarly behind a wall.
Both probably worth chasing through institutional access if available, but
not in this list.

I'm also leaving out the bin-packing / palletization-side literature
(BoxStacker, etc.). Container *un*loading and *loading* share some
geometry but the algorithmic problems are quite different — loading is
combinatorial optimization, unloading is sensing-driven sequencing — and
mixing the two muddies the reading list.

## Suggested Reading Order If You Have a Day

1. **Grasp Failure Constraints (2024)** — read first. This sets your
   gripper-side mental model.
2. **Dex-Net 3.0 (2018)** — read second. This is where the analytical
   suction-model lineage starts, and the multi-cup paper is much easier to
   parse afterwards.
3. **AI-Enhanced Parcel Detecting (2024)** — third. Establishes a baseline
   for the perception pipeline architecture.
4. **SCD paper (2021)** — skim. The dataset is the reusable artifact;
   the model is not.
5. **FoundationPose (2023)** — read only if pose estimation is in scope.
6. **Berkeley TR EECS-2019-119** — read for depth on the suction model
   if you want to actually re-derive their math.

## My One-Sentence Bet

If I were starting a deployable container-unloading prototype on a UR20 or
CRX-30iA tomorrow, the system architecture would be: classical depth-image
plane segmentation for box face detection, the multi-cup analytical QP
from arXiv:2408.03498 for grasp scoring and trajectory feasibility, and a
hand-rolled A*-style sequencer for pick order — with deep learning kept
on the bench as a "we'll add this once the deterministic system has been
running for three months and we have enough failure data to know what it's
actually missing." That's the inversion of how most academic papers frame
the problem, and it's also why most academic papers don't get deployed.
