Chapter 01 · Bolts in aluminium

A little thread.
A surprisingly large force.

Put a block of aluminium on the bench. Drill and tap a hole. Screw in an M8 steel bolt until 10 mm of complete thread is engaged. Now imagine gripping the bolt head and pulling straight upwards.

At first, nothing visible happens. The steel stretches a little, the aluminium deforms a little, and the thread faces press against one another. Increase the pull far enough and something must give. Will the bolt break? Will the aluminium threads strip? And how far below that point should we stay?

We will build a simple model to answer those questions. Keep this one example in mind as you read. Its numbers stay fixed throughout the chapter, even when you change settings in the lab below.

BoltM8, class 8.8 steel
Thread pitch1.25 mm
Engaged length10 mm
Aluminium5754-H111

M8 tells us the nominal outside diameter is 8 mm. Pitch is the distance from one thread crest to the next. With a 1.25 mm pitch, one complete turn advances the bolt by 1.25 mm. Our 10 mm engagement therefore spans eight turns.

A first, gentle calculation
Engaged turns = 10 mm ÷ 1.25 mm = 8

Eight turns is useful for picturing the connection. It does not mean each turn takes exactly one-eighth of the load. Real thread loading is uneven. Our model will estimate the connection as a whole.

NOTE

Engagement is the length where complete bolt and hole threads overlap. A 10 mm drilled hole usually gives less than 10 mm of full engagement because the drill point, tap lead and incomplete end threads use some space.

02 / GEOMETRYFind the metal that has to give way

To pull the bolt out without breaking the steel, you would have to shear away the aluminium teeth holding it. Imagine a thin cylindrical surface running through the engaged threads. Unroll that surface and it becomes a rectangle.

The rectangle is one circumference wide and one engagement length high. Circumference is π times diameter. We use the pitch diameter, d2, roughly halfway through the thread teeth, rather than the full outside diameter.

From nominal diameter to pitch diameter
d2 = d − 0.649519 × p
= 8 − 0.649519 × 1.25 ≈ 7.19 mm
d is nominal diameter; p is pitch. The decimal constant comes from the basic 60° metric thread geometry. It is not a material property or a safety factor.

The whole cylindrical surface is not solid aluminium resisting shear. NASA’s simplified tapped-hole model uses one-third of it as an effective resisting area. The factor accounts empirically for the thread geometry and imperfect mating. [1]

Effective thread shear area
Aeffective = π × d2 × L3
= π × 7.19 × 103 ≈ 75.3 mm²
We now have an area. The next question is how much force each square millimetre can resist.

03 / MATERIALGive that area a strength

A square millimetre of strong material can carry more force than the same area of weak material. This is what a stress value describes: force per unit area. In these calculations, 1 MPa means 1 newton per square millimetre.

Force capacity = resisting area × material strength.

For our 5754-H111 example, the viewer assumes a tensile strength Rm of 190 MPa. But the aluminium teeth are being sheared off, so we need a shear strength. The viewer estimates it as 60% of the tensile strength.

Estimate ultimate shear strength, then multiply
τultimate ≈ 0.60 × Rm = 0.60 × 190 = 114 N/mm²
Fstrip ≈ 75.3 mm² × 114 N/mm² ≈ 8,580 N
= 8.58 kN
τ (tau) means shear stress. F means force. A kilonewton is 1,000 newtons. Calculations use unrounded values internally.

Notice how the square millimetres cancel, leaving newtons. That is a useful check: an area multiplied by force per area must give a force.

Our model predicts aluminium stripping at about 8.58 kN. That is an estimated ultimate failure threshold. We have not yet chosen a sensible operating limit.

MODEL

The 0.60 conversion is an assumed approximation, not a measured shear strength for this plate. The result also depends on sound threads and enough surrounding aluminium. Real pull-out tests or verified design data are stronger evidence than this simplified estimate.

04 / MARGINStop before the connection is damaged

A connection can be damaged well before it comes completely apart. Yielding means permanent deformation: release the force and the material does not return completely to its previous shape.

The viewer therefore checks an earlier threshold. It takes the assumed tensile yield strength Rp0.2 of 80 MPa and estimates shear yield using the von Mises relationship:

An earlier limit: approximate shear yielding
τyield ≈ Rp0.2√3 = 801.732 ≈ 46.2 N/mm²
Fthread yield ≈ 75.3 × 46.2 ≈ 3.48 kN
Dividing by √3 translates tensile yield to a shear-yield approximation. It does not identify the first individual thread that will deform.

Now leave additional room between the applied force and that estimate. With the viewer’s screening factor n set to 2, the permitted force in this simplified check is halved.

Our example, with a screening factor of two
Fscreen = 3.48 kN ÷ 2 ≈ 1.74 kN
Number in this exampleWhat it describes
8.58 kNEstimated complete aluminium thread stripping
3.48 kNApproximate thread-yield threshold
1.74 kNThread-yield estimate divided by the chosen factor of 2

These three numbers answer different questions. That is why the large number in the lab is much lower than its “strip estimate.” The divisor of 3 in the geometry formula and the screening factor of 2 are separate: one belongs to the thread model, the other is an additional chosen margin.

05 / THE OTHER HALFRemember the bolt can fail too

So far, we have watched the aluminium. Now look at the steel. Threads cut into the bolt reduce its effective tensile area below that of a plain 8 mm rod. For a metric thread, the viewer calculates:

The bolt’s effective tensile area
Abolt = π4 × (d − 0.9382 × p)²
= π4 × (8 − 0.9382 × 1.25)² ≈ 36.6 mm²
The 0.9382 constant comes from the conventional effective stress-area expression for metric threads. Squaring the effective diameter makes this an area.

The assumed class 8.8 bolt has an ultimate tensile strength of 800 MPa and a proof stress of 580 MPa in this size range. Proof stress gives a reference for checking the bolt without relying on its breaking strength. [2]

Fbolt break ≈ 36.6 × 800 ≈ 29.3 kN
Fbolt proof ≈ 36.6 × 580 ≈ 21.2 kN

In our example the aluminium is the limiting part. A stronger steel bolt would not fix that. The lab always selects the smallest applicable capacity before applying the screening factor:

The complete direct-pull screening rule
Fscreen = min(Fthread yield, Fstrip, Fbolt proof)n
“min” means choose the smallest. For our example: min(3.48, 8.58, 21.2) ÷ 2 = 1.74 kN.

06 / DESIGN CHOICESMake it wider, or give it more depth

Return to the area equation: π × d2 × L ÷ 3. Diameter and engagement length each appear once. Double the length and the model doubles the thread capacity. Increase diameter by a quarter and the gain is roughly a quarter, with a small adjustment for the changing pitch.

Same aluminium; same factorEngaged turnsDirect-pull estimate
M8, 10 mm engaged8.01.74 kN
M8, 20 mm engaged16.03.48 kN
M10, 10 mm engaged6.72.18 kN

The M10 is wider, yet it engages fewer turns at the same depth because its coarse pitch is 1.5 mm. Counting turns alone misses the extra circumference of each thread.

Meanwhile, bolt tensile area depends on diameter squared. A bigger steel bolt gains tensile capacity faster than a shallow aluminium thread gains pull-out capacity. That is why increasing bolt size alone may leave the aluminium as the weak part.

Try it on the bench. Load the deeper M8 example and compare it with the wider M10. Watch the thread count as well as the force.

LIMIT

Linear growth is a simplification. Real threads load unevenly; extra depth eventually gives diminishing returns or makes another failure mode govern. The aluminium grade, available wall thickness and edge distance still matter.

07 / PRELOADTightening starts the load before the work does

Change the experiment. Put a plate under the bolt head and tighten it against the aluminium block. Tightening stretches the bolt slightly and compresses the parts. The bolt behaves like a stiff spring pulling its head and threads together.

Call this initial tension F0, the preload. Even with nothing pulling on the plate, the aluminium threads already carry F0. Preload is a force, measured in newtons or kilonewtons. Wrench torque is a different quantity; friction makes the conversion uncertain.

Now pull upwards on the plate with force P. The bolt tension increases, and the compression between the parts decreases. How much goes into each depends on the relative stiffness of the bolt and joint. The simple model calls the fraction added to bolt tension C. [3]

While the joint is still closed
Fbolt = F0 + C × P
Fclamp = F0 − (1 − C) × P
F0 · initial preload
P · external separating force
C · fraction added to bolt tension
1 − C · fraction releasing compression

Take a 1 kN preload and assume C = 0.20. If we apply another 0.5 kN to the plate, the bolt picks up 20% of that new pull, while 80% relieves compression:

Fbolt = 1 + 0.20 × 0.5 = 1.10 kN
Fclamp = 1 − 0.80 × 0.5 = 0.60 kN
Check the force balance: 1.10 kN bolt tension − 0.60 kN contact compression = 0.50 kN external pull.
0.50 kN

Fixed for this example: preload 1.00 kN; C = 20%. The bars each span 0–2 kN.

The joint stays closed. Some of the pull increases bolt tension; the rest releases compression.

Keep pulling. Eventually no compression remains. Setting Fclamp to zero gives the separation threshold:

When the joint begins to open
Pseparation = F01 − C = 10.80 = 1.25 kN

Above 1.25 kN in this example, the plate has lost contact with the block. The clamp force is zero and bolt tension equals the entire external pull. Do not keep adding the original preload on top after separation.

ASSUME

C = 20% is chosen to teach the mechanism. It is not a universal value for M8 bolts or aluminium. Joint geometry, stiffness and where the force enters the parts determine the actual load sharing.

For a joint that must remain closed, the lab stops at whichever comes first: separation or the selected bolt/thread screening limit. Solving the bolt-tension equation for the available external pull gives (Fscreen − F0) ÷ C.

Two conditions; the smaller permitted pull wins
Plimit = max(0, min(Pseparation, (Fscreen − F0) ÷ C))
≈ max(0, min(1.25, (1.74 − 1.00) ÷ 0.20))
= 1.25 kN
“max(0, …)” prevents a negative available pull. If preload already exceeds the screening limit, the result is zero. For this example, separation governs; staying firmly closed means operating below that boundary.

See the complete connection. Load the 1 kN preload / 0.5 kN pull example into the lab, then increase the pull until the plate separates.

08 / USING THE RESULTTurn the numbers into a design question

Our little M8 connection has led us to three useful distinctions: the geometry supplies the resisting area; the material supplies strength per unit area; and the load case tells us what force is actually reaching the threads.

When the aluminium governs, more sound engagement or a stronger suitable parent material can help. When the bolt governs, extra thread depth does little for the bolt’s tensile limit. When separation governs, the preload and joint stiffness need attention—but increasing preload also increases the force the aluminium must carry from the start.

The large figure in the lab is a screening estimate. It covers the simplified checks described here. It does not check edge breakout, thin-wall failure, bending, prying, head bearing, fatigue, shock or installation torsion. A chosen factor of two does not make those missing checks disappear.

Before using a result for a real part, confirm the actual material and temper, full engagement, surrounding geometry, load direction and tightening method. The kgf display is only a force conversion: it is not a lifting or supported-mass rating.

You can now read the lab’s numbers as a sequence: How much metal resists? How strong is it? What fails first? And how much of that capacity is already occupied by preload?

What to look for
01 / DIAMETER
A wider bolt has a wider thread cylinder.

Aluminium pull-out strength rises roughly with diameter. The steel bolt's tensile capacity rises roughly with diameter squared—so upsizing does not automatically fix shallow engagement.

02 / ENGAGEMENT
Deeper engagement adds shear area.

This simple model increases thread capacity linearly with full engagement, until the steel bolt governs. Real threads share load unevenly, especially at long engagement.

03 / PRELOAD
Tightening already pulls on the threads.

Preload stretches the bolt and compresses the joint. A separating load first relieves compression and adds some bolt tension. Once the joint opens, the bolt carries the whole applied pull.

Material assumptions, calculation, and sources

Listed materials 247TailorSteel lists 5754-H111 and 5083-H111 aluminium sheet. The latter is the stronger of these two plain-sheet grades, not the strongest aluminium available anywhere. Current stock and thickness must be checked in Sophia. The depth slider explores geometry; it does not indicate supplier stock sizes.

Calculation inputs 5754-H111: tensile strength Rm = 190 MPa, yield strength Rp0.2 = 80 MPa. 5083-H111: Rm = 270 MPa, Rp0.2 = 115 MPa. These are sheet/plate lower-bound inputs from thyssenkrupp data; the 5083 figures conservatively use the >6.3–80 mm thickness band. Exact product thickness, temper and material certificate still control.

Ultimate shear strength is estimated as 0.60 × Rm, not a measured supplier shear value. Shear yield is approximated by Rp0.2 / √3 (von Mises). “Yield” here is an engineering screening proxy, not a prediction of the first individual thread to deform.

All lengths in mm, stresses in MPa (= N/mm²), forces in N.
d₂ = d − 0.649519 × pitch
A_effective = π × d₂ × engagement / 3   [NASA empirical tapped-hole model]
F_strip = A_effective × (0.60 × Rm)
F_thread_yield = A_effective × (Rp0.2 / √3)
A_bolt = π / 4 × (d − 0.9382 × pitch)²
F_bolt_ultimate = A_bolt × 800   [class 8.8, M4–M16]
F_bolt_proof = A_bolt × 580
F_screen = min(F_thread_yield, F_strip, F_bolt_proof) / screening factor

Clamped joint: F_bolt = F₀ + C × P; F_clamp = F₀ − (1 − C) × P
Joint separation: P_sep = F₀ / (1 − C)
After separation: F_bolt = P; F_clamp = 0
Pull limit while remaining clamped = max(0, min(P_sep, (F_screen − F₀) / C))
1 kN ≈ 102 kgf. kgf is a force equivalent, not a supported mass rating.

The strip and break figures are separate modelled ultimate thresholds. Their minimum identifies the predicted failure mechanism. Working estimates additionally screen thread yielding and bolt proof load, and divide by the selected factor. The preloaded limit also stops at joint separation. A zero result means no additional separating load is permitted by that selected screening rule.
